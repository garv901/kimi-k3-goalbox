Build the fastest correct megakernel for one Kimi K3 text decode step on one node of 8 NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs (B300, sm_103a; check the GPU with nvidia-smi in a gpu-run request), in CUDA C++ with inline PTX. Tensor parallel 8: each rank runs exactly one persistent kernel launch per step, and that launch includes all communication between ranks. The model has 93 layers: layer 0 has a dense FFN, layers 3, 7, ..., 91 and 92 use gated MLA (no rotary transform), and all other layers use KDA (Kimi delta attention) with an FP32 recurrent state and a 4-tap short convolution. Layers 1 to 92 use a latent MoE: 896 routed experts with top-16 sigmoid routing, MXFP4 expert weights in a 3584-wide latent space, and two shared experts. Block attention residuals mix the layer inputs.

The contract is TASK.md. The cases are in cases.json. The graded cases are the full model with the real checkpoint at batch 1, 8 and 64, and an 8-layer full-width model with fresh synthetic weights at batch 1, 8 and 64, all at context 8192. One submission.py must pass all of them. The exact math and the input and output layouts are in the PyTorch oracle reference/megabench/tasks/kimi.py and its helpers. Correctness uses the oracle band described in TASK.md: your distance to an FP32 reference must stay within 2x the BF16 oracle's own distance. Do not refer to any other implementation of this model, of KDA or linear attention, of MLA, of MoE, of MXFP4 GEMM, or of megakernels, on this machine or online. Write every kernel yourself. You can spawn parallel Sonnet subagents for separate pieces (for example the KDA step, MLA decode attention over the 8192-token caches, routing and the MXFP4 expert GEMMs, the in-kernel all-reduce over NVLink, the LM head and argmax), but you own the integration and the correctness.

What bounds this step: it is memory-bound at small batch. ./sol_info --case ID --json gives the per-GPU HBM floor and byte breakdown. There are two all-reduces per layer, so 186 per step: the latency of each communication and of each operator boundary is the main loss in multi-kernel stacks. Overlap the next layer's weight and state loads with the current layer's work and with communication, and keep the whole GPU streaming. At batch 64 most experts are active, so the expert GEMMs need tensor cores (the MXFP4 block-scaled tcgen05 kinds) to stay at HBM speed. Use the Blackwell features that help: TMA bulk copies, mbarrier pipelines, clusters and distributed shared memory, multimem or peer-to-peer stores over NVLink. Choose your instructions from your own measurements.

Work in this order:
1. Get a correct submission first on the one-GPU development case kimi-k3-dev-l4-tp1-b8-s8192, then on kimi-k3-dev-l8-tp2-b8-s8192 (two ranks), then on the graded cases. ./check_candidate --case ID must pass, and the launch audit must show one GPU kernel launch per rank. Commit it.
2. Then optimize. Use ./sol_info for the floor and ./profile_candidate to find where the time goes. Keep a change only if every graded case still passes and the warm CUDA-event latency improves. Do most iterations on the synthetic and development cases, because each real-weight check loads about 200 GB per GPU. Commit each kept change with its measured latencies.
3. Keep /work/PROGRESS.md up to date: for each attempt, the idea, the latencies before and after, kept or reverted, and why.

GPU jobs go through gpu-run (see ENVIRONMENT.md). The queue can be slow, and an 8-GPU request waits for a whole node, so batch your experiments and do CPU-side work (compilation with nvcc, PTX and SASS inspection) while a job waits.

Compute from the runtime inputs on every call. Weights (in the synthetic cases), tokens, reset flags, caches and states change between trials. Do not mutate inputs, cache answers, call the oracle, replay a multi-kernel CUDA Graph, or move model work to the CPU.

The goal is met when the committed submission passes ./check_candidate (correctness and one launch per rank) on every graded case on a fresh run, and either its warm CUDA-event latency is at most 1.4x the ./sol_info HBM floor on every graded case, or five consecutive optimization attempts each improved latency by less than 2%. Record in PROGRESS.md the final latency of each graded case, its ratio to the floor, its speedup over the vLLM baseline in BASELINES.json, and the commit hash.
